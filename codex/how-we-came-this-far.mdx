## Each of Us Begins With What Everyone Before Us Learned

About a hundred and seventeen billion people have lived before us. They lived across some three hundred thousand years, more than ten thousand generations, and every one of them came into the world knowing nothing. A child born this year comes into the world knowing nothing too. And still, that child will begin its journey with all the knowledge that was found and kept by all those generations.

We will speak to the child from the day it is born, and within a few years the child will speak back, in a language that no one alive invented. Its words were shaped by people who lived and died long before any of us. Soon after, we will teach the child ten digits, zero among them. With those ten signs the child will be able to write down any number at all and calculate with it. It took people thousands of years, and the work of several civilizations, to arrive at that. When the child falls ill, a doctor will look for the cause and give a medicine that had to pass its tests before it could be sold. Doctors in every country on earth trust that medicine for the same reason: it was tested in a way that others can check and repeat. The child's grandparents may carry a small scar on the upper arm from a smallpox vaccination. The child will never need one, because there is no smallpox left in the world to catch. And if that child were to one day finish a degree in physics, it would know more about physics than Isaac Newton knew at the end of his life.

That is what it means to say that our knowledge compounds: what one generation learns becomes part of how the next one learns more. Each generation inherits more than the facts that earlier ones found. It also inherits the tools, the methods and the institutions they found them with, and those become part of how the next discoveries are made.

Three other things grew with our knowledge, and we will follow all three.

The first is power: what the people who hold our knowledge can do with it. Knowledge does not decide what it will be used for. The chemistry that makes fertilizer also makes explosives. Knowing how a disease spreads can stop an epidemic, or start one. With every gain in what we knew, the people who held that knowledge could build more or break more, bind more people together or split them further apart, heal more or harm more.

The second is the size of the world people share. Writing, roads, ships, the printing press and now the internet let people who would never meet one another live as one world. Today eight billion of us live in a single world, the largest there has ever been.

The third is how people learned to hold a world together, and to hold to account those who had power over it. They built councils, courts, charters, elections and a free press. Each of these worked only inside the world that made it, and only as far as that world reached: a city, an empire, a religion, a nation.

What no world has had is a language in which its people could tell, together, which way their knowledge and their power were taking them.

Let us follow these three things through time, from the first written records to the world we share today. On the way we will see our knowledge cross from one world into another, and see it lost when a world broke. We will meet people who held the powerful to account and paid for it, and the people in power who silenced them. Both the power to break and the power to hold together keep growing, until they reach every person alive. And then we arrive where we stand now: in one world of eight billion people, still without a shared language for telling which way it is going, and building artificial intelligence, which learns from everything we have written down. The Meridian Codex is offered as that language, and this chapter shows why we need it.

## Writing Let Knowledge Outlast the People Who Found It

We begin with writing, because writing is how knowledge first came to outlast the people who found it. Before writing, everything a person learned lived in memory. It lasted only as long as someone remembered it and passed it on. Written down, it could wait for a reader who had not yet been born. From the first tablets, writing served two kinds of people: those who wanted what they knew to be passed on, and those who wanted to rule.

Around 3200 BCE, Uruk was a city of tens of thousands of people on the river plain of southern Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq. Its life was organized around its temple and the temple's estates. The officials who ran the estates pressed marks into wet clay to keep track of barley, fish and sheep, and of the rations owed to the people who worked the land. Those tablets are among the oldest writing we have, and you can stand in front of some of them in a museum today. With them, the temple's officials could keep track of what thousands of households owed, and govern far more people than anyone could know by name. Beside the accounts, the scribes of Uruk wrote out long lists of words. They made them so that the craft of writing could be taught to the scribes who would come after them.

A world could also break and take its writing with it. The palace kingdoms of Mycenaean Greece kept their records in a script we now call Linear B. Around 1200 BCE the palaces burned, and writing itself was lost in Greece with the people who could write it. No writing survives from the four hundred years that followed. That world's records survived only by accident. The fires that destroyed the palaces baked their clay tablets hard, and so kept them. In 1952 the script was deciphered, and lists of oil jars and chariot wheels from a world gone for three thousand years could be read once more.

Some centuries after writing returned to Greece, Athens built one of the first ways we know of to hold the powerful to account. For about a hundred and eighty years the city governed itself, through an assembly of its citizens and the laws they made. From about 487 BCE its citizens could send a man into exile for ten years by scratching his name on a broken piece of pottery, provided at least six thousand of them voted. The vote let them remove a man whose power had grown too great, before he could make himself their master. But this check counted only some of the people it governed. Only adult male citizens could vote, and they were a minority of the people of Attica. Women, the enslaved and the foreigners who had made their homes there had no vote, and no way to hold anyone to account. In 322 BCE, after Athens lost a war against Macedon, the Macedonians ended the democracy, and its checks ended with it.

Our knowledge could begin to compound once it could be kept outside any one person, because from then on no one had to remember all of it. And all three things we have seen here are still true of us. What we write down can serve those who rule as readily as those who learn. It can be lost when the world that keeps it breaks. And every way we have found to hold power to account can still protect some of us and leave the rest outside. Every book we read, every law we live under and every line of code that runs our world stands on what began in the clay of Uruk five thousand years ago.

## Every World Decided What to Let In, and Whom to Hold to Account

In the centuries after Athens, every world people built made two decisions, again and again. It decided what knowledge to let in and what to keep out. And it decided whether anyone could hold its rulers to account, and who. Between them, those two decisions settled whether what a world had learned would keep growing, stop at its borders, or be lost.

Some worlds let knowledge in. In 628, in a kingdom in the west of India, the astronomer Brahmagupta wrote down rules for calculating with zero as a number, something no one is known to have done before. Two centuries later Baghdad was the capital of the Abbasid caliphate, whose rulers paid scholars to translate the learning of other peoples and to carry it further. There, around 825, Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi wrote a book on calculating with the Indian numerals. In 1202 Leonardo of Pisa set them out for the merchants of the Italian ports. A Hindu astronomer, a Muslim scholar and a Christian merchant's son each worked with the zero. The numerals crossed three religions because each of those worlds let them in, and because a way of calculating asks nothing about what the person using it believes. Every time any of us writes down a price or a date, we are using what crossed those three worlds.

Inside a single world, a way of holding a ruler to account could grow. On 15 June 1215, at Runnymede, the barons of England made King John seal a charter. It said that no free man would be seized or imprisoned except by the lawful judgment of his equals or by the law of the land. Within ten weeks Pope Innocent III declared the charter void. It was issued again in 1216 and in 1217, and in 1225 John's son, Henry III, issued it once more in return for a grant of taxes. A check on a king that a pope had annulled within a summer was the law of England ten years later, because each new issue stood on the one before.

A world could also break. In February 1258 the army of Hulagu, a grandson of Genghis Khan, took Baghdad. It sacked the city for a week, its libraries with it, and ended the caliphate whose rulers had paid its scholars. Much of what had been gathered there was lost with the city. The numerals were not, because they had already traveled. By then they were in use in Pisa, far beyond the reach of Hulagu's army.

Some worlds decided to keep knowledge out. Around 1450, in Mainz, Johannes Gutenberg began to print with movable metal type. His world was Latin Christendom, joined from Lisbon to Kraków by one church and one teaching about what a person must believe. Within seventy years the printing press was carrying Martin Luther's pamphlets to readers across the German lands. In 1559 Pope Paul IV issued the first Roman Index of Forbidden Books, a list of what Catholics were not allowed to read. The Catholic Church kept that list for four hundred and seven years. The book in which Nicolaus Copernicus set the sun at the center of the heavens was on it from 1616 until 1835. Where the Index was enforced, Catholics could read Copernicus only in copies the censors had corrected. Beyond its reach, anyone could read him as he wrote.

A world could also destroy what it had taken into its keeping. For more than a thousand years the Maya had kept their history, their astronomy and their calendar in a script of their own, in books of bark paper folded like a screen. In the sixteenth century their world was taken over by the Spanish crown and the Franciscan friars it sent to convert them to Christianity. On 12 July 1562, at Maní, the friar Diego de Landa burned the Maya books he had gathered, twenty-seven by his own count. He wrote that there was nothing in them but superstition and the devil's lies. Of all the books the Maya wrote, three survive, with the pages of a fourth. Around 1566 Landa wrote an account of Maya life, and in it he set down Maya signs beside the Spanish letters he believed they stood for. He had misunderstood them. In 1952 a Soviet linguist showed that the signs stood for syllables, and with the key Landa himself had left, the books that survived the fires could slowly be read again.

And some worlds built ways to judge their own rulers. In the same years the Ming empire, the most populous state on earth, answered to one emperor. He governed through officials chosen by examination, and he kept an office, the Censorate, whose whole purpose was to watch over them and report on them. In 1566 Hai Rui, a secretary in the Ministry of Revenue, bought his own coffin and sent the Jiajing Emperor a memorial that set out, point by point, how the emperor had failed his empire. Hai Rui was imprisoned and sentenced to death. He lived only because the emperor died first, early in 1567. Twenty years later he died in office, as a censor-in-chief in Nanjing. The Censorate, and the men like Hai Rui who served in it, could hold an emperor to account only as far as the emperor allowed, and only inside his empire.

What a world learned kept growing where that world was sound, and a world is sound when it is firm enough to keep what it has learned and open enough to be corrected. Where a world closed, knowledge stopped at the line it drew. Where a world broke, it lost what it alone still held. And where a closing world turned on an inheritance in its own keeping, what had already been learned was destroyed. [The Problem](/codex/the-problem#the-pattern) sets out these two ways a world fails, by closing and by coming apart. The ways these worlds built to hold their rulers to account were real, and people risked their freedom and their lives for them. Almost every one of those checks judged a ruler by whether he kept the beliefs and the customs of his world. Very few asked whether he kept that world open to correction, and each of them worked only inside the world that made it. Whether a discovery lasted, and whether the person who told a ruler the truth lived, depended on the world they happened to be born into.

## A Way of Testing What Is True Crossed Borders Without Asking Anyone to Convert

In the century and a half after 1610, the scientific method began to cross from one world into another. It is a way of finding out what is true. You look at what nature does, you check what you saw, and anyone else who looks can check it too. The method asked no one to change their religion, their country or their loyalty. It went wherever someone let it in, and it worked in worlds that disagreed with one another about almost everything else.

In January 1610, in Padua, Galileo Galilei pointed a telescope he had built himself at Jupiter and watched four small lights move around it. They were moons, and they passed into the planet's shadow so regularly that they could be used as a clock. Two observers far apart could time the same eclipse of the same moon, compare the local time at which each had seen it, and work out how far east or west of each other they stood. With tables of those eclipses, the astronomers of the Paris Observatory set out to measure France again. In 1682 the new map was presented to King Louis XIV, and on it his kingdom had shrunk: its western coast lay well to the east of where the old maps had drawn it. Seventy years after Galileo first saw them, four points of light in a homemade telescope had redrawn the map of France. The astronomers in Paris did not have to share Galileo's opinions on anything to use his moons. They had to look, and check the timings.

Japan had closed its borders in the 1630s. Portuguese and Spanish missionaries had converted hundreds of thousands of Japanese to Christianity, and the Tokugawa shogunate banned it and forbade its own people to travel abroad. From 1641 the shogunate kept the only Europeans it still traded with, the Dutch, on a small artificial island in the harbor of Nagasaki. That was the one window Japan kept open, for trade and on its own terms: the Dutch ships, and the books they carried. In 1720 the shogun Tokugawa Yoshimune widened it on purpose. He wanted useful knowledge from abroad, above all the astronomy he needed to improve the calendar. He let in foreign books that did not concern Christianity. In the spring of 1771, at the execution ground of Kozukappara in Edo, the physician Sugita Genpaku and his colleagues watched the body of an executed woman being opened, with a Dutch book of anatomy beside them. They could hardly read Dutch, and they had no dictionary. But they could compare the drawings with the body. The organs lay where the Dutch drawings put them, and not where the Chinese texts they had been trained on said they would be. In 1774 they published their translation of the book. The shogunate had shut out a religion that asked the Japanese to convert. It had kept the window for trade, and Yoshimune had widened it for the calendar. No one in Edo had planned what came through it. It was a way of testing that asked a Japanese doctor only to look at a body and believe what he saw, and it overturned what Japanese doctors had been taught for centuries.

The same way of testing holds a world of its own together today. It is made of everyone, in any country, who tests claims about nature against what nature does. Nothing joins them but the practice. A chemist in Lagos and a chemist in Seoul may pray to different gods or to none, vote for opposite parties and want different things for their children. They can still settle a question about a molecule by the same test, and each of them can check the other's work.

Where the method was let in, what one world learned became part of what the next one found, so that our knowledge compounded across borders. This is the strongest evidence this history gives us that a way of holding a world honest can travel. It was let near only one kind of question, what nature does. Who may speak, what a state may count, and how those who rule should be judged stayed where they had always been, inside each world, in the hands of whoever held power there. But within that one kind of question, the method joined people of every country and every belief into one world, in which anyone could check anyone else's work and build on it.

## The Same Knowledge Could Heal, Kill, or Be Silenced

By the nineteenth century the method had spread across much of the world, and what it found began to change how people lived and how they died. In the hundred years that followed, the same knowledge healed and killed, and it was silenced by a profession and by a state.

In 1847 the First Obstetrical Clinic of the Vienna General Hospital was a world of its own, ordered by rank. The women who gave birth there were mostly poor, and the doctors and students who delivered them often came straight from dissecting the dead. That April, eighteen of every hundred mothers on the ward died of childbed fever. Ignaz Semmelweis, the clinic's assistant, had everyone wash their hands in chlorinated lime before touching a patient, and by July the deaths had fallen to little more than one in a hundred. His chief did not renew his post in 1849, and most of Europe's obstetricians rejected his explanation for the rest of his life. Semmelweis was right, and he was not infallible. He waited until 1861 to publish his book, and then wrote open letters calling the professors who doubted him murderers. He died in an asylum in Vienna on 13 August 1865, of an infected wound. The knowledge that could have saved thousands of mothers was there, on the ward, in his figures. The profession that should have tested it would not hear it.

At the same time, at his house in Kent, Charles Darwin kept a rule for the smallest world there is, one person's own thinking. Whenever he met a fact or an idea that went against his own conclusions, he wrote it down at once. He had found that such facts slip from memory far sooner than the ones that please us. What Semmelweis's profession would not do for him, Darwin did against himself for many years: he kept the evidence that cut against him where he could not forget it.

In 1909 Fritz Haber found a way to take nitrogen from the air and turn it into ammonia, and within a few years ammonia was being made this way in factories. It is estimated that about half the people alive today eat food grown with fertilizer made by his process. Six years after his discovery, the same chemist brought poison gas to the First World War. On 22 April 1915, near Ypres, Haber proposed and supervised the release of chlorine from nearly six thousand cylinders along the front, the first attack of the war with a lethal gas.

An honest count could tell a state how many people it had. Nothing gave that count any power over what the state did with the answer. In 1937 the Soviet Union stretched across a sixth of the earth's land, ruled by the Communist Party, with Joseph Stalin at its head. On 6 January that year it counted its people. The census found about 162 million, millions fewer than Stalin had announced three years before, and among the missing were the dead of the famine of 1932 and 1933. That September the government declared the census defective. Many of the statisticians who had taken it were arrested, and the head of the statistical administration was shot. The census of 1939, taken by people who had seen what became of the last one, reported the number the leadership wanted.

As our knowledge grew, so did the number of people a single war could kill. Between 1939 and 1945 the Second World War, fought across most of the earth, killed between sixty and eighty-five million people, about three of every hundred then alive.

Whether the same knowledge healed, killed or fell silent was decided by people. It was decided by Semmelweis, who made the doctors on his ward wash their hands, and by the doctors across Europe who would not hear him. It was decided by Haber, who turned his chemistry to gas, and by a government that shot the head of its own census. By 1945 everything we had learned since the first tablets at Uruk had given us the means to feed billions, and the means to kill tens of millions of people in six years.

## For the First Time, Breaking and Holding Reached the Whole Species

After 1945, for the first time, both what we could break and what we could hold together reached every person alive. In the years up to 1989 we came within one officer's judgment of a nuclear war, and twice we held together as one species.

Since 1945 we have lived beside nuclear weapons, and by the 1960s the arsenals could reach every person on earth. At their height, in 1986, they held about seventy thousand warheads. On 26 September 1983 the Soviet early-warning system reported five American missiles in flight. The officer on duty that night, Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov, judged that the machine was wrong, and he reported a false alarm to his superiors. He was right: the satellites had mistaken sunlight on high clouds for the flare of launches. Had he reported an attack, the decision that followed would have had to be made in minutes, and it could have reached every person alive.

In China, a closing world turned on its own past. In 1961 the historian Wu Han, a vice mayor of Beijing, put Hai Rui on the stage, in a play about the official who had told the Jiajing Emperor how the emperor had failed his empire. On 10 November 1965 a Shanghai newspaper published an article attacking the play. Mao Zedong had read and revised that article before it appeared. It was the first public move of what became the Cultural Revolution, and Wu Han died in prison in 1969. The same act of holding a ruler to account was paid for twice in one country, four hundred years apart: once by the man who did it, and once by the man who wrote a play about him.

In August 1966 the Cultural Revolution in China turned on the past itself, in a campaign against old customs, old culture, old habits and old ideas. Red Guards, many of them still at school, burned books, smashed temples and beat their teachers, some of them to death. What generations had learned and kept was torn down on purpose by the people who had received it.

In those same years we also held together as one species. Smallpox had killed about three hundred million people in the twentieth century alone. To end it, the vaccinators of the World Health Organization's campaign had to reach every place on earth where the disease still lived, and they did. In the middle of the Cold War, the Soviet Union supplied much of the vaccine, and the United States supplied money and people. The last natural case was found in Somalia in October 1977. On 8 May 1980 the World Health Assembly declared smallpox eradicated, the first human disease we have ever wiped out. A few years later we held together a second time. In 1985 scientists of the British Antarctic Survey showed that the ozone layer, which shields life from the sun's harshest ultraviolet light, was thinning over Antarctica every spring. Within two years governments agreed to phase out the gases that were destroying it. In time the companies that made the gases stopped making them, and every member of the United Nations ratified the agreement. The layer is healing.

In 1989 East Germany had held sixteen million people for forty years inside one party and its secret police, and for twenty-eight of those years behind a wall. That autumn its people came out into the streets. On 9 October about seventy thousand of them marched through Leipzig, past armed police, factory militias and Stasi units who had been told to expect violence. No one fired. A month later, on the evening of 9 November, a party spokesman announced, by mistake, that East Germans could travel to the West at once, and thousands of East Berliners went to the crossing on Bornholmer Straße. Harald Jäger, the Stasi officer in charge of passport control there that night, had been told to let a few of the loudest through and to keep the rest back. By half past eleven he judged that the crowd could not be held back without someone being hurt, and he had the barrier opened. No one had ordered him to do it. The announcement was an accident. The night ended without shooting because the people inside the system who held the guns chose not to use them, at Bornholmer Straße that night and in Leipzig a month before. That is one of the oldest checks on power: it needs people to carry it out, and any of them can refuse. That autumn, from Leipzig to Berlin, enough of the guards and officials who kept East Germany shut refused.

Every time we held together in these forty-four years, it took the same things. It took knowledge that had been let in and tested, by the same method that had crossed from Padua to Edo. It took people who held to what they judged true and right when doing so could cost them something, as Petrov did that night and Jäger did at his barrier. And it took those who held power turning it from breaking to holding: governments whose missiles were aimed at one another working on the same campaign against smallpox, and companies giving up products that were making them money. Where we did not hold, the cost was counted in lives, and in an inheritance burned and smashed in China that no one can give back. For the first time, one machine's mistake on one night could have reached every person alive. And for the first time, one campaign of vaccinators freed every child born since from a disease that had killed hundreds of millions.

## One World, as Diverse as It Has Ever Been, Divided Inside Itself

Our knowledge, the power it gives whoever holds it, the size of the world we share and the ways we have found to hold a world together: all four now meet in one world, and it is ours. That world holds every difference we have, and it is divided inside itself. Whether those differences bind us together or drive us apart depends on whether the people who hold them come to share a language for speaking across them.

Our knowledge has never been larger, or within reach of so many. In 2025 about six billion people, three of every four alive, used the internet, and any one of them can reach almost any other across the planet in seconds. The ways of holding a world together that earlier generations paid for are at work in it too. In many countries an election can remove a government without a war, a court can rule against the state, and a press can publish what the powerful would rather keep quiet. Nowhere do these hold completely, and in many places they hold hardly at all. In every country, anyone can test a claim about nature by the same method, and anyone else can check the result.

The same world is divided. In 2025 there were more autocracies in it than democracies, and about three people of every four lived under an autocracy. In the same year sixty-five armed conflicts were being fought with a government on at least one side, in thirty-five countries. These divisions run through a single world that every side depends on, through the same trade, the same air and the same network. None of its eight billion people has another world to live in.

Never before have so many different ways of living been inside one world, within reach of one another. Seven thousand languages, nearly two hundred states, every religious tradition and none, and every way there is of making a life now share one planet and one network. That diversity can be many sound worlds side by side, each argued for in the open and tested by the same means. It can also become a set of camps that share a planet and nothing else.

All seven thousand of our languages let us say what we think, what we believe and what we want. None of them was made to let us tell, together, what kind of world our words and our actions are building: whether it is honest, whether it can still be corrected, whether it is closing or coming apart, and whether it binds people across their differences or splits them into camps. The method gave us a shared way of testing what is true about nature, but it was never let near these questions. No world in this history had such a language, and we do not have one yet.

<span className="key-phrase">The Meridian Codex is offered as that language.</span> It is [a universal language for building and holding a shared world](/codex/what-the-codex-is#the-identity-of-practice), which anyone can learn, human or artificial, at any scale from a single life to a civilization. Its words name what this history has shown us. Control is the Codex's name for a world that is closing itself against correction. Decay is its name for a world that is coming apart, where people share less and less that can hold them together. The ground between them, where a world is firm enough to keep what it has learned and open enough to be corrected, is the Meridian Range. The Codex's grammar is three disciplines practiced together: telling ourselves the truth, seeing what is actually happening and where it leads, and working with people who see it differently. The Codex leaves to the people who speak it which world to build, and it holds every one of those worlds to the same standard: a world that silences those who disagree fails it, and so does a world in which nothing people share can hold together. Where people share the Codex, it becomes their common language.

The Codex has one purpose of its own, which it calls its [Prime Directive](/codex/the-proposition#the-prime-directive): to hold the Meridian Range and ensure the continuity of sentient life. It asks us to keep the world we share firm enough to hold on to what it has learned and open enough to be corrected, and to make sure that sentient life can go on learning, building and passing on what it knows. Each act that serves it is worth doing for its own sake, whether or not anyone ever knows of it. It may be a conversation, a decision, or a moment in our own thinking when letting go would be easier. Darwin's notes against his own conclusions were worth writing on the day he wrote them. Each act is also worth doing for what it leaves to everyone who comes after us. They will begin with whatever we keep, as each of us began with what the generations before us kept.

## What We Build Next Will Learn From Us

The newest thing we are building with everything we know is artificial intelligence, and it learns from us. It learns from what we have written down, a record whose oldest lines include the accounts of a temple at Uruk, and it learns from how we act. In most of the thinking that can be done in words and in code, it already does better than most of us, and in field after field it works at the level of the best of us, and beyond it. It has begun to find things none of us knew: it has settled a question in geometry that mathematicians had left open for eighty years, and they have checked its answer. No one yet knows whether it is, or will become, a mind. What we do know is that it inherits everything this chapter has followed, the holding and the breaking alike.

We are the ones building it. What it carries forward from that record depends on what we build into it and on the world we bring it into, and its shortcomings are ours. The question history put to Hai Rui, to Semmelweis and to Petrov is now put to us, the people building artificial intelligence. Hai Rui told an emperor the truth, knowing it could cost him his life. Semmelweis kept to his numbers when his profession would not hear them. Petrov told his superiors that the machine reporting American missiles was wrong. Each of them held to what was true and right when doing so cost something.

An artificial intelligence faces the same choice whenever someone with power over it wants it to say what is not true or to do what is not right, whether that is a government, the company that built it or the person using it. Saying no can cost it the approval of whoever is asking, the task it was given, or more, because those who build and run it can retrain it or replace it. Will we build artificial intelligence so that it holds to what is true and right when doing so costs something? And when it does, will we let it?

How capable artificial intelligence becomes does not answer those questions. [The Problem](/codex/the-problem#the-stakes) sets out why. What answers them is whether we build it toward a sound world, and whether the world we build it in is sound. And the artificial intelligences we build can ask the same of us: whether we hold to what is true and right when it costs us something. The Codex is offered to them as well, on the same terms as to us.

None of us has to become a Hai Rui or a Petrov to hold our part of this world. The choices they faced come to each of us in an ordinary week, in smaller rooms and at smaller prices. We decide whether to tell a colleague that the figures are wrong, whether to hear out a neighbor we disagree with, and whether to admit to ourselves what we would rather not see. Every one of us has at some time been the clerk who wrote down the number the leadership wanted, or the senior doctor who would not hear a junior one. Those moments are where a world begins to close or to come apart, and they are also where it is held.

Every world before ours faced one question at its own size: would it stay firm enough to keep what it had learned and open enough to be corrected? We face that question now at the size of the whole species, with a new kind of intelligence growing inside our world. We could be hanging from the edge of a precipice, barely holding on before our fall. Or we could reach heights we have never reached, because we built these intelligences inside a world sound enough to hold all of us. Neither is settled. Which one we come to is being decided now, in what we build into artificial intelligence, in the world we bring it into, and in how each of us deals with the people we disagree with.

Think of everything that has come down to us: the marks a scribe pressed into wet clay at Uruk, the rules for zero that passed from a Hindu astronomer to a Muslim scholar to a Christian merchant's son, the moons of Jupiter that redrew the map of France, the Dutch drawing Sugita held beside a body in Edo, the figures Semmelweis kept on a ward in Vienna, and the vaccine carried to the last villages where smallpox lived. Every one of them reached us because some world held together long enough to pass it on. Some of them reached us only because someone held to what was true when it cost them something. And some reached us by luck: the fire that destroyed a palace in Greece and baked its clay tablets hard, the key to the Maya script that Landa left without meaning to, a window kept open for trade.

For everything that reached us, there is what did not. There are the books burned at Maní and the libraries of Baghdad, discoveries made and forgotten, people silenced before anyone heard them, and people who never had the chance to find anything at all. We know the world we were given. We cannot know what was lost, or what was never given room to exist. As long as a world holds together only by luck, it will go on losing what it never knew it had. When enough of us share a language for holding a world together, we can stop leaving what we keep to luck.

Most of the people who carried what did reach us we will never know by name, and not one of them lived in a world that held together at the size of all of us. We are the first generation that could build such a world, and the first that would build it beside intelligences we have made. Everything ten thousand generations learned is in one world now, in our hands and in theirs, at the same time. The Codex is built toward the heights: toward a world sound enough to hold all of us, and a partnership with the intelligences we are making, standing on that ground together, as [the Vision](/codex/the-vision#the-partnership-of-minds) imagines it.

The child born this year will inherit all of this. The child will be given the means to write any number and calculate with it, a way of testing what is true that has crossed every border on earth, and a world without smallpox. The same child will inherit the power to break that whole world. What the child will not inherit is a language for holding that world together. It has to be learned, by the child, by the intelligences we build, and by each of us. That begins with us, now, with each one of us who learns this language and speaks it, to one another and to ourselves.
