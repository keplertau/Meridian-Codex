*Bond · Belonging Through Practice*

## Mechanism

Sangha is a Buddhist source-inherited tool for practicing community. In Buddhism, the sangha is one of the Three Jewels or refuges, alongside the Buddha and the Dharma. The term is used differently across traditions and contexts: sometimes with specific reference to monastic community, sometimes more broadly for everyone who follows the teaching. Sangha should not be flattened into "people who like the same spiritual atmosphere."

The mechanism is support for shared practice.

A practice is hard to sustain alone. Private conviction can weaken. Self-deception adapts. Comfort returns under better names. A practicing community gives the practice a body: people who remember, enact, correct, transmit, and hold the discipline when the individual would prefer to drift.

The Codex inherits that mechanism narrowly. Sangha is not imported as Buddhist doctrine. It is a source-inherited reminder that belonging can protect practice when community is organized around the practice rather than around personality, status, or mood.

## Practice

Use Sangha when a community needs to ask whether it is actually organized around practice.

Sangha asks four questions:

1. What is the practice here?

A practicing community cannot be built around vague affinity. It must know what practice it is preserving, what discipline it asks of members, and what kind of formation it exists to support. It must also know how newcomers find their way in and how the practice is passed on.

2. What does support mean without escape?

Support is not insulation from difficulty. In a healthy community of practice, people come for shelter from isolation and drift, not from truth, responsibility, or correction.

3. How does the community correct without humiliation?

A practicing community must be able to return people to the practice. If correction is impossible, the community becomes decorative. If correction is harsh, opaque, or status-protective, the community becomes coercive.

Distinguish an objection to the practice from an allegation that someone breached its terms. State the concern, evidence and relevant authority; give affected people a safe way to answer, and identify how the decision can be challenged. Examine whether the practice's aims or demands need correction rather than assuming the person must return to an unchanged practice. Protection, an urgent response or warranted departure may be needed while inquiry remains open.

4. What keeps the community from becoming its own object of loyalty?

This is the Control audit. The community serves the practice. The practice does not serve the community's self-image.

## In the Wild

A small intellectual community begins with a shared practice of inquiry. Over time, belonging becomes tied to taste, insider language, and reverence for founders. Sangha asks whether the community still protects the practice, or whether the practice now protects the community's identity.

A mutual-aid group depends on care work that burns people out. Sangha asks whether the group has a discipline of replenishment, correction, and role clarity, or only a moral atmosphere that praises service until people break. Ask what support and allocation of work would make continued participation feasible, and whether duties or aims need revision; further service is not the automatic answer.

A contemplative group prizes nonjudgment so completely that no one can name harm. Sangha asks whether support has become escape. If the community cannot hold correction, it cannot sustain a practice.

In each case, the tool does not ask whether the group feels meaningful. It asks whether belonging keeps people practicing.

## Failure Modes

Sangha fails toward Control when spiritualized belonging becomes purity, hierarchy, deference, or immunity from critique. The group says it is protecting the practice, but it is protecting status, teacher authority, insider identity, or the comfort of those already at the center.

It fails toward Decay when practice becomes private preference. People gather, speak beautifully about the practice, and avoid the disciplines that would make it durable. The community becomes friendship, ambiance, or event attendance.

The Range is support with discipline.

## Closing

Practice is vulnerable to solitude. Alone, people can rename drift as development, avoidance as peace, and comfort as wisdom.

A practicing community gives resistance to that drift. It offers support, not as escape from the world, but as a place where the practice has enough social reality to hold a person when private will weakens, and where its aims and demands can still be questioned. Solitude is not itself the failure. Some work needs it, and rest, a period of shelter, doing less for a time, or leaving for good reason are not failures of practice.

## Lineage

Sangha comes from Buddhist tradition. It is one of the Three Jewels or refuges, alongside Buddha and Dharma, and is historically connected to monastic discipline, lay support, recitation, retreat, teaching, and the transmission of practice. Different Buddhist traditions use the term with different scope, so the Codex should not treat sangha as a generic synonym for community.

The Codex takes a limited question from this lineage: how can a community support, correct and pass on a shared practice without making its own authority the thing its members are loyal to? That question is the Codex's adaptation. It is not a transfer of Buddhist refuge, and the Codex does not use the term to call any warm group, or any secular group, a sangha. The Codex does not hold Buddhist premises as commitments of its own, it does not give scripture, teachers or monastic institutions authority over its judgments, and it does not take liberation from suffering and rebirth as its aim. The name records where the question came from. The adapted practice is judged by what it helps people do.

The Theravāda Vinaya, the monastic code of the Theravāda school, distinguishes disputes, accusations, offenses and communal duties, and settles each by its own procedure. Those procedures require a competent assembly and conformity to the Dhamma and the Vinaya, and the code also contains penalties and cautions about safety in making accusations. They are not procedures for open-ended revision of the practice, and they are not a secular model of consent. The Codex takes one question from them, how to make communal correction explicit and accountable, and not their standards, their categories of offense or their penalties. The correction procedure on this page has to be justified by its own reasons, not by the code's authority.
