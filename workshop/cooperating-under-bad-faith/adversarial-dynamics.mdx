<div className="flow-label">01 // Mechanism</div>

## Mechanism

Adversarial Dynamics is the lens that makes predation, capture, and exploitation legible inside cooperative systems. Inside Cooperating Under Bad Faith, it is the lens the category's other tools operate under: trust mining, the cooperative vulnerability, the exclusion problem, and sabotage diagnostics are each a specific mechanism this lens brings into focus.

Most of what the Codex maps as failure is drift. Incentives misalign over time, entropy accumulates, and systems move toward Control or Decay because no one is holding them against the current. That kind of failure builds up through diffuse pressure, though the break it produces can come slowly or all at once; the river erodes the bank. Some failures arrive differently. An actor sits inside the cooperative framework, learns enough of its language to pass its calibration tests, accumulates authority through visible alignment, and then spends the accumulated authority on something the framework was never designed to sanction. The cooperative vocabulary itself — good faith, charitable engagement, the strongest version of your argument — becomes the instrument of the exploitation, because those are the tools the framework runs on. This is a river diverted upstream by someone who knew exactly which channel to cut.

<span className="key-phrase">Some failures involve deliberate exploitation of structural conditions, rather than drift alone.</span> The existing Codex analysis of drift is correct, and it is also incomplete. A framework for cooperation that does not account for the actors who come prepared to exploit it reads, to anyone who has watched exploitation happen in real time, as a framework for sincere people in a world that is not only sincere.

The adversary in this model is not the caricature. They can be intelligent, fluent in the cooperative system's language, sometimes more fluent than most of its members, and convinced they serve some higher purpose. The pattern is strategic rather than malicious in the ordinary sense: the actor studies the cooperative system, learns its norms, and uses that fluency to accumulate authority specifically in order to spend it. This requires not paranoia but recognition: the incentive to pass for cooperative is available to anyone in any system that rewards people for looking cooperative, and any framework aimed at continuity has to account for that.

Structural drift is the nearest boundary, not a second adversary inside this tool. Incentives can reward defection and produce exploitative outcomes through people who never adopted an exploitative strategy. Those conditions require systems diagnosis and repair. They enter this lens only when a participant begins to use the resulting asymmetry strategically or invokes cooperative norms without accepting reciprocal constraint. Deliberate disruption is not wrongful in itself: before treating it as exploitation, name the justified cooperative function it threatens. Disrupting an unjust practice on purpose is not exploitation.

What the lens brings into focus is a set of specific mechanisms. Each is a contributing tool in this category with its own profile; what follows is what the lens reveals about each.

**Trust mining** is the practice of building up trust capital specifically in order to spend it. The accumulation phase can look like an ordinary career inside a cooperative system: the actor passes calibration tests, demonstrates alignment, earns authority through visible competence, patiently, sometimes over years. Then the accumulated authority is spent: extraction, redirection, sometimes outright capture. The two phases can overlap, with misuse beginning while the trust is still being built. The specific vulnerability this reveals: calibrated trust, the Bond's own prescription, can be gamed by an actor patient enough to pass the calibration. Institutional capture can involve deliberate use of accumulated trust, or arise through incentives and dependencies without that strategy. Trust Mining names the former when evidence supports it; capture alone does not establish it.

**The cooperative vulnerability** is the framework's own vocabulary becoming the attack surface. Steelmanning turns into a legitimation tool; good faith becomes a shield demanded asymmetrically; connection before correction becomes a guarantee that a manipulative position will be received sympathetically before it can be challenged. Cooperative language is genuine when both parties are accountable to it. It is weaponized when one party uses it to constrain the other while exempting themselves. The test is whether each party is accountable to the terms that apply to them. The answer is not to abandon good faith but to make discretionary cooperation, access and exposure conditional on reciprocation, and to build the diagnostic capacity to see when reciprocation is being performed rather than practiced. Representing the other party's claims accurately is not part of what becomes conditional.

**The exclusion problem** is the hardest operational question the lens raises: when does a cooperative setting have to exclude a participant in order to survive? The Compact says identity is through practice, but it does not turn that identity into an institutional membership status or authorize anyone to exclude a person from practicing the Codex. The downstream question is whether a participant's systematic conduct is consuming the conditions that let others practice together in a particular setting. A serious, well-supported risk can justify prompt, proportionate protection. Four conditions frame a settled decision to exclude from that setting — a sustained pattern of bad faith or a single grave, well-supported incident, evidence of strategic exploitation distinct from honest disagreement, repair attempted where it can be done safely, and an exclusion that serves the Range rather than the excluder's comfort. The risk of false positives is severe: labeling dissent as sabotage is one of the oldest Control moves in institutional history, and exclusion wielded to enforce conformity is the Codex practicing the failure mode it claims to resist.

**Sabotage diagnostics** is the discipline of telling genuine dissent from strategic sabotage. From outside, the two can be indistinguishable: both oppose prevailing positions, both name problems, both are uncomfortable for the institution on the receiving end. Context and cumulative evidence can help distinguish the explanations; no time interval guarantees the diagnosis. Five behavioral signatures, used as questions about the case, help organize that evidence.

<div className="flow-label">02 // Practice</div>

## Practice

The diagnostic question is: **"Is the other party accountable to the same norms they are invoking?"**

Three practices convert that question into something you can apply in the middle of a live situation.

<div className="glass-card">

**The Reciprocation Test.** Before extending further trust or engagement, ask whether the other party is accountable to the norms they are invoking. If they demand good faith but do not practice it, or insist you steelman their positions while engaging yours in caricature, that asymmetry is worth examining. The test is not whether they use the cooperative vocabulary but whether the vocabulary binds them as it binds you. Look for evidence of strategic use or unjustified asymmetric accountability, and compare accidental, role-based and structural explanations. Asymmetry raises a question; it does not itself establish bad faith. Prevention or protection can be warranted while the strategic explanation remains uncertain.

**The Pattern Assessment.** Single interactions are ambiguous, and patterns over time can stay ambiguous too. Track whether the actor's behavior consistently produces fragmentation where cooperation was possible, whether their arguments shift with the audience in ways that suggest strategic positioning, whether they engage the strongest versions of opposing positions or reliably the weakest, and whether they respond to evidence or only to changes in strategic position. Record the context, the instances that cut the other way, and what you have not been able to see. Compare the strategic explanation with the others, including justified criticism and a defective procedure. More observations help when each one adds independent information; no particular count or stretch of time establishes what is causing a pattern. Say how confident you are and what would change your mind. The Bond's commitment to cooperate in good faith does not require you to ignore what patterns over time reveal about who you are actually cooperating with.

**The Structural Check.** Keep prompt protection separate from a settled judgment that someone is exploiting the cooperative system. A serious risk that the evidence supports well can justify a proportionate restriction before the conduct repeats and before any repair is complete. Record the evidence, who decided, how far the restriction reaches and why, and have the decision reviewed promptly by someone independent of it whenever the setting can provide such a person. The settled judgment, and particularly exclusion, needs more: evidence that supports "this person is exploiting the cooperative system" rather than only "this person is difficult," corroboration from people independent of the case wherever you can get it, the other explanations weighed, and a fair way for the person to challenge it. Take that judgment slowly, because the cost of false positives is as severe as the cost of false negatives; protection does not wait for it.

</div>

These three practices share an architecture. Each converts an intuition — *something is off here* — into a structural claim: this is the asymmetry, this is the pattern, this is the evidence. The conversion is where the discipline lives, because intuitions on their own go wrong in both directions: too generous toward the exploiter, too harsh toward the dissenter. The practices slow judgment down and require it to surface its reasoning, which is what lets you examine the reasoning before you act on it.

## In the Wild

Sam Bankman-Fried founded the cryptocurrency exchange FTX in 2019 and became one of the best-known donors in effective altruism, a movement built on rigorous argument, charitable engagement and the careful allocation of resources, whose language he spoke fluently. FTX collapsed in November 2022. A federal jury in New York convicted him of fraud and conspiracy, and in March 2024 he was sentenced to 25 years in prison. The U.S. Attorney's Office for the Southern District of New York describes the scheme as running from 2019 to 2022, with billions of dollars in customer deposits channeled from FTX to his trading firm, Alameda Research. The record does not show a clean honest phase followed by a late turn: by the prosecutors' account, the misuse of customer money ran through the same years in which his standing as a donor grew. Nor does it show that the movement's norms caused the fraud. It does raise the questions this lens asks: what authority his visible alignment bought, what he represented and to whom, who had access to the money, and who was checking it.

The next three cases are composites, drawn to show a pattern rather than to report a particular event. A regulatory agency was created to oversee a powerful industry. Over two decades, senior staff rotated through industry positions and back into the agency. Each individual career move was defensible, several admirable on their own terms. The aggregate was capture: the agency stopped producing decisions that constrained the industry in meaningful ways. On the facts given, no adversarial threshold has been crossed. Career incentives, informational asymmetry, and repeated interaction explain the outcome without strategic use by a participant. The case belongs first to systems diagnosis. It would enter this lens only if an actor began knowingly using those conditions to extract value or invoke the institution's rules asymmetrically.

A political movement adopted the language of reform. Its members attended every meeting, followed every procedure, won every vote. Critics were told to engage through the established processes. When objections were raised through those processes, the movement used its majority to block them. When procedural changes were proposed to restore balance, the movement invoked the sanctity of process. When the process produced outcomes the movement did not want, the movement attacked the process. Each move on its own was defensible; the pattern added up to sabotage. What was consistent was not any substantive position but the selection rule underneath it — the appeals shifted with the strategic position, and the commitment to process held only for as long as the process produced the wanted outcome.

A small team operating with high mutual trust noticed something odd. One member's contributions were increasingly received as correct by default, while another member's were increasingly examined carefully. When the team sat down with it, the asymmetry had nothing to do with the quality of the work — it was about accumulated authority. The first member had been demonstrating visible alignment for months; the second had been disagreeing, legitimately but uncomfortably, with roughly the same frequency. The team had quietly started treating visible alignment as evidence of competence. They found no strategic use or reciprocation failure, so they repaired the calibration drift without attributing bad faith. The example marks the lens's lower boundary: asymmetry is a prompt to investigate, not proof of an adversary.

<div id="closing" className="flow-label">04 // Closing</div>

The Bond without adversarial dynamics is incomplete: it teaches how to cooperate well, and it has to also teach what cooperation looks like when the person across from you has studied the cooperative framework in order to take from it.

Suspicion as default would destroy the cooperative framework as thoroughly as any adversary could — it would turn you into the thing you were resisting and cost you the advantages cooperation gives you in the first place. Naive openness fails in the opposite direction, inviting over time exactly the exploitation it refuses to anticipate. What the Bond asks for under adversarial pressure is harder than either: calibrated trust that carries a specific new capacity inside it, the ability to notice, while it is happening, the signs that the cooperative framework itself is being turned against you, and to weigh them against the other explanations. That capacity is not native. It has to be built.

The Codex values disagreement and requires dissent; nothing in adversarial-dynamics analysis reduces that commitment. What it changes is only the assumption that everyone invoking the cooperative vocabulary is also accountable to it. Whether they are accountable, not whether they speak the language, is what you are watching for.

## Lineage

The Codex did not invent adversarial dynamics. The mechanisms named on this page draw on several independent research traditions of the twentieth century; the Codex's contribution is to assemble them into a coherent lens for the Bond. The practices on this page are the Codex's adaptations of that work, not results those traditions established.

Robert Axelrod's computer tournaments, analyzed with the biologist W. D. Hamilton in *Science* in 1981 and in Axelrod's *The Evolution of Cooperation* (1984), laid the game-theoretic groundwork. In repeated two-player games with fixed payoffs, conditional strategies like Tit-for-Tat outperformed both unconditional cooperation and unconditional defection, and the analysis showed the conditions under which cooperation breaks down: when the shadow of the future shortens, when players cannot recognize one another or remember past moves, when a player is about to leave the game. Trust mining extends that logic beyond what the tournaments modeled. It assumes that authority accumulates inside an institution over years, and that the payoff from a single large defection can grow until it exceeds the value of continued cooperation. Those assumptions are the Codex's own; the tournaments do not establish them.

Mancur Olson's *The Logic of Collective Action* (1965) and Elinor Ostrom's *Governing the Commons* (1990) developed the institutional economics of cooperation. Olson's work on free-riding named the underlying exposure: in a large group that shares a common benefit, each member gains by letting others pay for it, so contribution tends to fail without coercion or selective incentives. The step from free-riding to defecting while appearing to cooperate, and extracting value before anyone detects it, is the Codex's extension. Ostrom's work on commons governance mapped the institutional defenses — graduated sanctions, monitoring designed into the structure, clearly defined membership — the ancestors of the practices on this page.

The political science literature on entryism and regulatory capture documents two neighboring routes to capture. Entryism supplies strategic cases in which actors enter an institution in order to redirect it. George Stigler's work (*The Theory of Economic Regulation*, 1971) shows that regulatory bodies can also come to serve the industries they regulate through career incentives, informational asymmetry, and repeated interaction. The second route is the boundary: capture can exist without bad-faith strategy, so capture alone does not satisfy this tool.

Karl Popper's *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945) stated the paradox of tolerance: unlimited tolerance, extended even to those who are intolerant, leads to the destruction of the tolerant and of tolerance with them. Popper claimed the right not to tolerate the intolerant, and held that suppressing them is unwise as long as they can be countered by argument and kept in check by public opinion. John Rawls' *Political Liberalism* (1993) developed the idea of reasonable pluralism: a liberal society may not use its power to repress reasonable doctrines, but it does not need the agreement of unreasonable doctrines and may contain them so that they do not overturn political justice. The exclusion problem is a direct descendant of this line, adapted from political philosophy to cooperative frameworks.

These political-philosophy discussions concern the justified limits of toleration and the terms of reciprocal cooperation. They do not grant a general permission to withdraw all consideration or protection from a person who does not cooperate. In section 35 of *A Theory of Justice*, Rawls concludes that an intolerant sect has no title to complain of intolerance, and yet that its freedom should be restricted only when the tolerant sincerely and with reason believe that their own security and that of the institutions of liberty are in danger. The Codex adapts the question to participation in a particular cooperative setting.

The wider social-psychology and institutional literature is a boundary warning rather than evidence of a second adversary. Structures can shape behavior and produce harmful or extractive outcomes without bad-faith strategy. Adversarial Dynamics should not convert that general fact into motive attribution. Its narrower job begins when strategic use, patterned reciprocation failure, or asymmetric accountability is visible in conduct.

## Cross-references

**Within the category.** Adversarial Dynamics is the lens; the other four contributing tools in Cooperating Under Bad Faith are the specific mechanisms it brings into focus. [Trust Mining](/inheritance/cooperating-under-bad-faith/trust-mining) is the accumulation-then-extraction pattern, whose phases can overlap; institutional capture belongs to it only where accumulated trust is used deliberately. [The Cooperative Vulnerability](/inheritance/cooperating-under-bad-faith/cooperative-vulnerability) is the framework's own vocabulary turned into an attack surface. [Sabotage Diagnostics](/inheritance/cooperating-under-bad-faith/sabotage-diagnostics) is the discipline of telling genuine dissent from strategic sabotage across a pattern of behavioral signatures. [The Exclusion Problem](/inheritance/cooperating-under-bad-faith/exclusion-problem) is the conditions under which a participant has to be excluded from a particular cooperative setting for that setting to survive. Each has its own profile; this lens carries the compressed treatment that frames them as one architectural family.

**Across the Inheritance.** [Calibrating Trust to Behavior](/inheritance/calibrating-trust-to-behavior) is the Bond category Adversarial Dynamics most directly pressures — calibrated trust is the Bond's prescription, and trust mining is the strategy that games the calibration. [Receiving Disagreement Well](/inheritance/receiving-disagreement-well) carries the receiving-side function of Steelmanning, which Adversarial Dynamics shows can be weaponized when one party engages the strongest version of their own position while caricaturing yours. In the Foundation, [Steelmanning](/inheritance/revising-beliefs-under-evidence/steelmanning) itself is the clearest case of a cooperative practice that becomes an attack surface under bad faith — the lens reads the asymmetry that Steelmanning's own practice cannot detect from inside.

**Two limitations worth naming.** Adversarial-dynamics reasoning can become paranoid in the wrong hands. A framework that treats every disagreement as potential sabotage has already failed the test it claims to apply; the tools here are built to raise the threshold for adversarial interpretation, not lower it. If you find yourself reaching for this lens to explain away dissent you happen to find uncomfortable, you are using it wrong. Second, no diagnostic eliminates uncertainty. You can apply every signature, follow every practice, and still be wrong about a specific case. The tools make the evidence and the competing explanations visible, so that your reasoning can be checked before you act on it; they do not produce certainty, and they do not establish anyone's intent. The position this profile takes is that imperfect tools applied with discipline are better than no tools applied with confidence.
