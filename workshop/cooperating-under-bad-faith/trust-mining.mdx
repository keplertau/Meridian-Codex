<div className="flow-label">01 // Mechanism</div>

## Mechanism

Trust Mining is the practice of building up trust capital specifically in order to spend it. The pattern has two phases, and they need not be cleanly separate: misuse can begin while trust is still being built, alongside contributions that are real. The first is accumulation: the actor passes calibration tests, demonstrates alignment, earns authority through visible competence, patiently, sometimes over years. The second is extraction: at the point where the accumulated authority is high enough to be worth spending, the actor redirects the institution's resources, norms, or mission toward an outcome the framework was never designed to sanction.

What makes the pattern hard to catch is that the accumulation phase can be indistinguishable from an ordinary career inside a cooperative system. The behaviors that produce earned trust — passing calibration tests, demonstrating alignment, contributing visibly, building relationships, accumulating credibility through results — are the same behaviors the system was designed to reward. From inside the accumulation phase, the actor looks like a high-functioning member who is doing exactly what the system asks. Early cooperation can look like ordinary contribution, and some warning signs may be absent or missed. Review the evidence and safeguards without assuming either hidden strategy or guaranteed detectability.

<span className="key-phrase">The accumulation phase is the cost of the extraction phase, paid in advance.</span> A long, visible, expensive career inside a cooperative system is the price of admission to a level of authority worth spending. Trust mining is what an iterated-defection strategy looks like when the rounds are years long and the payoff from a single large defection exceeds the discounted value of continued cooperation. The model extends Axelrod's repeated games to a case his tournaments did not test: an actor whose time horizon is long enough to absorb the cost of cooperation as a calibration expense. That extension is the Codex's own.

Capture is the institutional neighbor of this pattern. Senior staff rotate through industry positions and back into the regulatory agency, or through advocacy organizations and back into the institutions those organizations were created to oversee. Each individual career move is defensible. Many are admirable on their own terms. The aggregate effect is that the institution stops producing decisions that constrain the captured interest in meaningful ways, while the formal structure persists for years after the substantive function has been hollowed out. Institutional capture can involve deliberate use of accumulated trust, or arise through incentives and dependencies without that strategy. Trust Mining names the former when evidence supports it; capture alone does not establish it. Either way, the capture is invisible step by step because step by step nothing unusual is actually happening. It is the pattern, integrated across years, that produces the outcome.

The specific vulnerability the mechanism reveals is structural: calibrated trust, the Bond's own prescription, is gameable by an actor patient enough to pass the calibration. The calibration runs on behavior over time, and a sufficiently patient actor can produce behavior over time. The defense is not to abandon calibrated trust — that returns the framework to either naive openness or suspicion as default — but to add a layer of pattern recognition that operates on the career-arc scale, not on the within-interaction scale where calibration normally runs.

## Practice

The diagnostic question is: **"Does the alignment-demonstrating behavior I am rewarding fit a pattern consistent with what an accumulator would do, regardless of present intent?"**

The question is not whether the actor is currently accumulating trust strategically. That question is hard to answer from outside, and asking it without evidence is often the move that turns a legitimate institution paranoid. The question is whether the system's calibration is running on a time horizon short enough that a patient actor could game it without ever producing a signal the calibration can see. Three practices test the calibration itself.

<div className="glass-card">

**The Phase Diagnostic.** Ask what the authority permits, what evidence supports relying on this person in that domain, and what has changed since the trust was first extended. Then ask over what horizon the trust is being calibrated. Calibration on within-interaction or within-month behavior is easy to game for an actor with a multi-year plan. Calibration on years of behavior is harder to game, though not impossible. A longer history makes the judgment better informed, but it cannot reveal a strategy that has not yet shown itself, so ask also whether oversight and constraints keep pace with the stakes of the authority being extended.

**The Inflection Question.** Ask at what level of accumulated authority spending the authority becomes more attractive to a rational actor than continuing the accumulation. The question is structural, not personal. A position with $200 million in customer deposits but no external monitoring has a different inflection point than a position with $200 million in customer deposits and continuous external audit. Surface the inflection point as a property of the position, then ask whether the system's monitoring intensifies as the position approaches the inflection point or whether the monitoring stays constant. Systems where monitoring stays constant as authority grows have built a payoff structure that rewards trust mining, and the reward grows with the authority. That is a risk to reduce, not a prediction that fraud must follow.

**The Career-Arc Pattern.** Look at the actor's career arc as well as the current moment. Trust mining is legible at the arc scale even when it is invisible at the moment scale. The arc questions: did the actor's apparent priorities change after authority was acquired? Did the resources under the actor's control move in directions that benefit constituencies the framework was not designed to serve? Did the actor's external relationships during the accumulation phase predict the direction of the extraction? Read that history, those relationships and the changes in the resources the person controls alongside their current conduct and access, and keep the innocent explanations in view. Look only as far into a person's history as the authority at stake justifies; nobody owes an institution their whole life story. A well-supported breach in the present can justify action without any long career pattern.

</div>

The three practices share a discipline: they operate on the system's calibration architecture rather than on any individual actor's behavior. The shift in object — from "is this person trustworthy" to "is this calibration gameable" — is what keeps the tool from drifting into the failure mode it most resembles. Unsupported suspicion can make a setting paranoid. Evidence-based inquiry into conduct or strategic use can be warranted; preventive design does not require resolving private intent.

## In the Wild

FTX, the cryptocurrency exchange Sam Bankman-Fried founded in 2019, came to hold billions of dollars in customer deposits, and its founder became one of the best-known donors in effective altruism, a community built on rigorous argument, charitable engagement and careful allocation. His authority and the money under his control grew together; the checks on them did not. According to the U.S. Attorney's Office for the Southern District of New York, the scheme to defraud FTX's customers ran from 2019 to 2022, the same years in which his alignment with the community was most visible, so the record does not show a long honest phase followed by a single turn. When John J. Ray III took over FTX in bankruptcy, he described a complete failure of corporate controls. The case shows the architecture this tool names: calibration running on the alignment dimension, none running on the inflection-point dimension, and a position whose stakes kept growing while the checking stayed where it was.

A regulatory agency was created to oversee a powerful industry. Over two decades, senior staff rotated through industry positions and back into the agency. Each individual move was defensible. The aggregate was capture: the agency stopped producing decisions that constrained the industry in meaningful ways. On the facts given, this is the boundary case rather than Trust Mining. No one strategically accumulated trust in order to extract it; career incentives, informational asymmetry, and repeated interaction produced the outcome. The system still needs repair, but the diagnosis belongs first to institutional drift. It becomes Trust Mining only where an actor deliberately uses the legitimate channels and accumulated authority as an extraction strategy.

A small team operating with high mutual trust caught its own drift. One member's contributions had been received as correct by default for months while another member's were increasingly examined carefully. The asymmetry had nothing to do with the quality of the work. It was about accumulated authority. The team had quietly started treating visible alignment as evidence of competence. They surfaced the pattern before it could institutionalize, and corrected it by making the basis for their assessments explicit. They correctly treated the pattern as calibration drift rather than Trust Mining because they had no evidence that the authority was being accumulated or used strategically. The same architecture would cross this tool's threshold if the trusted member began exploiting the default credibility while resisting equivalent scrutiny.

<div id="closing" className="flow-label">04 // Closing</div>

The defense against trust mining is not vigilance about individual actors. It is calibration architecture that operates on horizons long enough to detect the pattern and that intensifies its monitoring as authority approaches the inflection point. The Bond's commitment to calibrated trust is not weakened by adopting the tool; the tool operationalizes the commitment at the scale where calibration has to work against a patient adversary.

What the tool asks of you is to treat the calibration as a designed object rather than as a posture. A posture cannot defend against a multi-year accumulation strategy. A calibration architecture that runs on the right time horizon and intensifies where the stakes intensify can. This is what calibrated trust requires once you accept that some of the actors the framework will encounter have come prepared.

Adversarial Dynamics supplies the lens, and The Cooperative Vulnerability names the practice-language attack surface. Trust Mining contributes the temporal pattern: accumulation that buys authority and extraction that spends it, sometimes in sequence and sometimes at once. It belongs here because ordinary trust discipline needs a career-arc and inflection-point check when authority itself becomes the thing being mined.

## Lineage

Robert Axelrod's *The Evolution of Cooperation* (1984) established the game-theoretic foundation. Tournament results showed that cooperative strategies outperform defection across iterated interaction under specific conditions: a long shadow of the future, players who can recognize one another and remember past moves, and the possibility of retaliation against defection. Trust mining, as the Codex models it, is the defection strategy that exploits the conditions themselves. The actor pays the cost of cooperation for long enough to make the payoff from a single large defection exceed the discounted value of continued cooperation, and then defects in a domain or at a scale where retaliation cannot be calibrated against the accumulated trust.

Mancur Olson's *The Logic of Collective Action* (1965) and Elinor Ostrom's *Governing the Commons* (1990) developed the institutional economics of the vulnerability. Olson's work on free-riding named the underlying exposure: in a large group that shares a common benefit, each member gains by letting others pay for it, so contribution tends to fail without coercion or selective incentives. The step from free-riding to defecting while appearing to cooperate, and extracting value before anyone detects it, is the Codex's extension. Ostrom's work on commons governance mapped the institutional defenses — graduated sanctions, monitoring designed into the structure, clearly defined membership — that the practices on this page descend from.

George Stigler's *The Theory of Economic Regulation* (1971) documented a neighboring institutional-capture form. Regulatory bodies can come to serve the industries they regulate through career incentives, informational asymmetry, and repeated interaction rather than strategic conspiracy. That literature identifies the calibration vulnerability and the legitimate channels through which capture can proceed. It is a boundary source, not evidence that every captured institution has been trust-mined.

The behavioral-economics work on patience and time-discounting underlies the structural claim about adversarial time horizons. Actors with longer effective time horizons can absorb larger calibration expenses, which means systems with payoff structures that grow over time without proportionate monitoring increases are systems that select for patient adversaries.

## Cross-references

**Within the category.** Trust Mining operates under the lens of [Adversarial Dynamics](/inheritance/cooperating-under-bad-faith/adversarial-dynamics) — the category's adversarial threshold and its diagnostic question of whether the other party is accountable to the same norms they are invoking. Trust Mining and [The Cooperative Vulnerability](/inheritance/cooperating-under-bad-faith/cooperative-vulnerability) are structural siblings: Trust Mining names the temporal architecture of strategic accumulation; The Cooperative Vulnerability names the rhetorical architecture of weaponized cooperative practice. The two run in tandem in many real cases, with accumulated trust providing the platform that the asymmetric invocation of cooperative norms operates from.

**Across the Inheritance.** Calibrating Trust to Behavior is the Bond category Trust Mining most directly games — calibrated trust is the Bond's prescription, and Trust Mining is the strategy designed to pass the calibration. The relationship is structural rather than oppositional: Trust Mining does not refute calibrated trust as a discipline; it specifies the calibration architecture calibrated trust requires once a patient adversary is in the population. The reframing the lens performs on Calibrating Trust to Behavior — adding the inflection-point and career-arc dimensions to the standard within-interaction calibration — is what the lens delivers to that category's practice.

**Limitations.** Two worth naming. First, Trust Mining analysis can drift into reading every long career as a potential accumulation strategy, which produces exactly the suspicion-as-default failure the category is built to refuse. The defense is the shift in object the Practice section names: the question is the calibration architecture, not access to the actor's private intent. Second, an accumulation-and-extraction pattern can emerge without strategic design when a person drifts under structural pressure. That is a serious systems failure, but it does not become Trust Mining until the evidence supports strategic accumulation or exploitation. The diagnostic vocabulary should not collapse adverse outcomes, incentive drift, and bad-faith strategy into a single account.
