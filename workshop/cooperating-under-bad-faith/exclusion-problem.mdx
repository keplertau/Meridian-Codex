## Mechanism

The Compact says identity and belonging are through practice. Its boundaries are practice boundaries — *are you doing the work?* — rather than identity or belief boundaries. It does not turn that identity into an institutional membership status or authorize an institution to decide who may practice the Codex.

A community or institution practicing the Codex still has to decide who may continue participating in that particular cooperative setting. A participant can systematically violate the practice while continuing to invoke it. Not occasionally — each of us sometimes falls short, and the framework holds that as part of practice rather than as grounds for exclusion. Systematically. The pattern across many encounters shows that the participant's conduct is consuming the conditions that let others practice together. Trust Mining shows one version of the pattern; the Cooperative Vulnerability shows another; Sabotage Diagnostics catches a third. When the pattern has accumulated to the threshold those tools name, the setting reaches a question it cannot answer by extending more cooperation: can it survive this participation, and on what terms? The answer governs participation in that setting. It does not determine whether the person may practice the Codex elsewhere, or whether repair and return remain possible.

<span className="key-phrase">Exclusion wielded to enforce conformity is the framework practicing the failure mode it claims to resist.</span>

The history of cooperative frameworks is largely the history of frameworks that drifted into using exclusion as a tool for enforcing conformity rather than for protecting the cooperative substrate. Every regime, every captured organization, every closed movement that calls itself open has at some point excluded critics in language the framework's own architecture provided. Both wrongful exclusion and continued harmful access can cause severe or irreversible loss. Examine the evidence, harms and feasible options rather than assuming either error can always be repaired. A framework that has institutionalized the practice of excluding inconvenient critics has become the closed system the Codex's diagnosis is built to refuse.

The exclusion problem therefore has two failure modes. The first is naive inclusion: a cooperative setting so committed to openness and repair that it cannot exclude even actors whose participation is destroying the practice itself. The second is weaponized exclusion: a setting that has learned to use the language of bad-faith detection to remove participants whose practice is inconvenient to its current direction. The tool is structurally calibrated to refuse both, and the four conditions are the operational expression of that calibration.

Keep prompt protection separate from a settled decision about who may continue in the setting. A serious risk that the evidence supports well can justify a proportionate restriction before the conduct repeats and before any repair is complete. Record the evidence, who made the decision, how far the restriction reaches and why. Protect the people affected, and have the decision reviewed promptly by someone independent of it whenever the setting can provide such a person. A single grave incident, well supported, can also justify a longer exclusion when lesser restrictions, or a repair that could actually be carried out, would not protect the setting. Explain that judgment, and give the excluded person a fair way to challenge the evidence. Repair is attempted when it is safe and has a real chance of working. It never requires unsafe contact, and it never requires the participation of the person who was harmed. Criticizing an exclusion is not itself grounds for removal. Exclusion from one setting does not remove the protections a person has whether or not anyone trusts them, such as being described truthfully and treated without abuse.

For a settled decision about participation, four conditions frame the judgment. None alone is sufficient.

**A sustained pattern of bad faith, or a single grave incident.** Most single incidents are ambiguous. Some are bad-faith conduct; others are honest disagreement that landed badly, a difficult day, a misread context, a critic who is uncomfortable to engage but operating in good faith. Pattern over time usually carries the diagnostic load, and the pattern requirement is what protects critics whose individual moments are sharp but whose arc is constructive. A grave, well-supported incident is the exception: it does not have to repeat before the setting may act.

**Evidence of strategic exploitation distinct from honest disagreement.** Sharp critics, persistent dissenters, actors who hold positions the framework finds uncomfortable — none of these qualify. What qualifies is evidence that the actor's behavior fits the architecture of strategic exploitation: the trust-mining pattern, the cooperative-vulnerability pattern, the sabotage pattern, or some combination, with the diagnostic work already completed by the relevant tools. A single grave incident can meet this condition when the evidence shows the act was deliberate exploitation and not a misjudgment or an honest dispute. The condition rules out exclusion based on discomfort, ideological inconvenience, or institutional preference.

**Repair attempted where it can be done safely.** The framework's commitment to repair after rupture is operational. When a participant's conduct has produced rupture, the framework's standard practice is to attempt repair: acknowledgment, accountability, sometimes restitution, the re-establishment of terms under which cooperation can resume. Exclusion is ordinarily downstream of repair failure, not parallel to repair. Where repair can be attempted safely, the condition specifies that it has been attempted in full and has failed against an actor who is not pursuing repair as practice. Where repair would not protect the setting, a grave case does not wait for it to fail. This is the most procedurally demanding of the four conditions, because the framework's commitment to repair is not weakened by adversarial dynamics — it is precisely tested by them.

**An exclusion that serves the Range rather than the excluder's comfort.** The Range-serving condition is the discipline against weaponized exclusion. The question is structural: will excluding this participant protect the cooperative substrate, or will it remove a participant whose presence is uncomfortable to those running the exclusion? The two often look similar from inside; the diagnostic is whether you can articulate the structural protection in terms a critic of the exclusion could engage substantively, and whether the framework would accept the same standard applied to your own conduct.

For a settled exclusion the four conditions are conjunctive: the framework reaches that decision responsibly only when each has been answered on the evidence. Requiring all four is what raises the threshold high enough that the tool can be operational without becoming the failure mode it exists to refuse.

<div className="flow-label">02 // Practice</div>

## Practice

The diagnostic question is: **"Have all four conditions been honored, and does the exclusion serve the Range or the excluder?"**

The question is structural and procedural. It runs on evidence the framework can produce and inspect, and the burden of proof sits on the side of exclusion rather than on the side of inclusion. Three practices operationalize it.

<div className="glass-card">

**The Conditions Audit.** Run each of the four conditions independently against the case at hand. For each condition, produce specific evidence that satisfies it, with citations to observable behavior and to the relevant diagnostic findings (Sabotage Diagnostics for the first two conditions, repair-process records for the third, structural articulation for the fourth). The audit is documentary rather than impressionistic. A condition that cannot be evidenced is a condition that has not been met, and a condition that has not been met is sufficient to refuse exclusion regardless of how strong the other three are.

**The Repair-Completion Verification.** Where repair can be attempted safely, verify that the full repair sequence has actually been attempted, not merely gestured at. Acknowledgment of the rupture in specific terms. Accountability surfaced and engaged. Restitution where it was warranted. The re-establishment of cooperative terms attempted with the participant in good faith. The verification fails if the framework gave up on repair early, if the repair process was procedurally truncated, or if the framework's own conduct during repair was itself the failure point. Exclusion downstream of failed repair is structurally different from exclusion in lieu of repair. The tool honors the second only where repair would be unsafe or could not protect the setting, and the record has to say which.

**The Structural Safeguards Check.** Before any exclusion is enacted, run the safeguards against weaponized exclusion: procedural transparency about the conditions found and the evidence supporting them, corroboration and scrutiny from people independent of the case, articulation of the structural protection the exclusion provides in terms a substantive critic of the exclusion could engage, and your willingness to have the same standard applied to your own conduct. Seek that corroboration whenever you can, and judge the evidence by its quality, by what the observers could actually see, and by what it would cost the people involved to come forward, not by how many observers there are. A well-documented record can justify action without several eyewitnesses. Say what remains uncertain, and give the decision a review that matches what is at stake. The check is not a delay tactic. It is the operational form of the framework's commitment to not become what it is excluding.

</div>

The three practices share a discipline that is uncomfortable by design: they slow the exclusion process down at exactly the moments when slowing it down is hardest. A framework experiencing real harm from a bad-faith actor wants to move quickly. The tool slows the settled decision to exclude, because a setting that learns to exclude quickly has institutionalized the failure mode it was built to refuse. It does not slow protection: a restriction can come as fast as the harm requires, with its evidence, reasons and review recorded. Moving slowly does not by itself make a decision careful.

## In the Wild

Take the recurring shape of scientific-fraud cases. A long-time member of a scholarly community is found to have falsified experimental results across multiple publications over several years. The four-condition test runs against the shape. The pattern is sustained, not a single incident. The fabrication is strategic — the member's career has been built on the fraudulent results, and exposure carries personal cost they have repeatedly worked to avoid. Repair is attempted: the community's processes for retraction, accountability, and re-engagement are offered. In the cases where the member declines to retract and continues defending the work, the repair sequence completes in failure. The exclusion that follows is structurally clean: the four conditions are honored procedurally, the evidence is public, and the protection the exclusion offers the community is articulable in terms outside critics could engage. Real instances along this arc tend to leave their communities internally divided on edge questions about exclusion's scope. The procedure holding up under scrutiny does not by itself show that the decision was right; that rests on the evidence of fabrication, which is public and can be checked.

A political faction inside a movement called for the exclusion of a critic who had repeatedly disagreed with the faction's strategic direction. The Conditions Audit, run in good faith, produced a finding the faction did not want: the pattern was sharp disagreement, not bad faith. The critic engaged the strongest version of opposing positions, offered alternatives, accepted the movement's processes even when they ruled against them, and updated under evidence. None of this was required of the critic; it was simply what the record showed. The faction's exclusion call did not survive the audit. The movement had encountered the failure mode the tool exists to refuse and caught it before it consummated. The critic remained inside the movement; the faction's conduct in calling for exclusion became the next item in the movement's own self-audit.

An open-source project encountered a contributor whose pattern, surfaced over six months across multiple maintainers, fit the Cooperative Vulnerability mechanism precisely. Demands for charitable interpretation of their own contributions were paired with uncharitable engagement of others'. Procedural objections appeared whenever the project's processes produced unwanted outcomes. Repair attempts by maintainers were met with reframing in which the maintainers became the problem. The project ran the Conditions Audit, completed the Repair-Completion Verification, and ran the Structural Safeguards Check. The exclusion that followed was lean, evidenced, and procedurally transparent. The project's other contributors — including critics of the project's general direction who had been adjacent to the case — accepted the exclusion not because they agreed with every decision the project had made but because the procedure had been visible and the standard applied was one they would accept being held to themselves. Their acceptance was evidence that the decision was legitimate; it did not settle the question on its own.

<div id="closing" className="flow-label">04 // Closing</div>

The exclusion problem is the hardest operational question the category raises because it is the place where the framework's commitments meet the survival of a particular cooperative setting. The Compact's identity-through-practice commitment, the Bond's commitment to repair, and the Foundation's commitment to charitable interpretation all remain operational. They have to encounter the rare case in which a participant's continued conduct would consume the conditions that let others practice together. Exclusion sets a boundary on participation in that setting. It does not redefine the Compact or decide who may practice the Codex.

The four conditions are the framework's answer. They do not eliminate the difficulty of the question. They specify the discipline that allows the question to be answered honestly. A setting that can run the conditions audit, attempt repair in full wherever it can be done safely, and pass the structural safeguards check can make the decision to exclude that participant from that setting. A documented process makes the reasons inspectable; it does not establish that they are sound. Examine necessity, authority, proportionality, alternatives and the affected person's fair challenge. A setting that excludes without honoring the four conditions is not protecting the cooperative substrate. It is becoming the closed system the diagnosis began with.

<div className="glass-card">

**Why this tool is in the Inheritance.** The Exclusion Problem is here because cooperation sometimes has to protect itself, usually after repair has been tried and failed, and sometimes when repair cannot safely be tried. Repairing After Rupture carries the prior obligation; Sabotage Diagnostics supplies the pattern evidence; Belonging Through Practice keeps the Compact's identity-and-belonging-through-practice architecture visible. This tool belongs only at the downstream edge: exclusion is not punishment, abandonment, or a shortcut around repair. It is the guarded question of whether continued participation in a particular setting is consuming the cooperative conditions the framework exists to protect.

</div>

## Lineage

Karl Popper's *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945) stated the paradox of tolerance: unlimited tolerance, extended even to those who are intolerant, leads to the destruction of the tolerant and of tolerance with them. Popper's formulation is the conceptual ancestor of the tool, with one specific refinement carried forward: Popper himself argued that tolerance of the intolerant should end only where the intolerant refuse to engage on rational terms. The condition is procedural, not substantive. The Exclusion Problem inherits the procedural form — exclusion is conditioned on observable failure of the cooperative process, not on the substantive content of the participant's positions.

John Rawls' *Political Liberalism* (1993) developed the idea of reasonable pluralism: a liberal society may not use its power to repress reasonable doctrines and must justify its basic terms in ways all reasonable citizens could accept, but it does not need the agreement of unreasonable doctrines and may contain them so that they do not overturn political justice. Those who hold them keep their rights as citizens. The Rawlsian distinction between reasonable and unreasonable disagreement is the political-philosophy form of the line between sharp dissent (which the framework requires) and exploitation of the framework's cooperative substrate (which the framework cannot indefinitely absorb).

These political-philosophy discussions concern the justified limits of toleration and the terms of reciprocal cooperation. They do not grant a general permission to withdraw all consideration or protection from a person who does not cooperate. In section 35 of *A Theory of Justice*, Rawls concludes that an intolerant sect has no title to complain of intolerance, and yet that its freedom should be restricted only when the tolerant sincerely and with reason believe that their own security and that of the institutions of liberty are in danger. The Codex adapts the question to participation in a particular cooperative setting.

The institutional theory on legitimate banning across organizations, professions, and online communities informs the safeguards on this page: procedural transparency, corroborated evidence, an attempt at repair where it is safe, and a standard others can see applied. These are proposed Codex safeguards whose adequacy depends on the case, evidence, risks and available process; they are not established universal requirements across institutions.

The historical record on weaponized exclusion — across regimes, organizations, religious communities, and political movements — provides the calibration that justifies the tool's high threshold. The rhetoric of "protecting the community" has been used to suppress inconvenient critics with such frequency and across such diverse contexts that any framework willing to enact exclusion must build the safeguards against this failure mode into the tool itself. The four conditions, the documentary requirements, and the structural safeguards check are the direct response to that record.

## Cross-references

**Within the category.** The Exclusion Problem operates under the lens of [Adversarial Dynamics](/inheritance/cooperating-under-bad-faith/adversarial-dynamics), which carries the threshold between supported strategic use and nonstrategic drift that the category's tools share. The most direct upstream relationship is with [Sabotage Diagnostics](/inheritance/cooperating-under-bad-faith/sabotage-diagnostics), which produces the kind of pattern finding that usually answers the Exclusion Problem's first two conditions. For a settled exclusion on grounds of sabotage, Sabotage Diagnostics comes first, run with discipline. Prompt protection from a serious, well-supported risk does not wait for that finding, and the two can run together. [Trust Mining](/inheritance/cooperating-under-bad-faith/trust-mining) and [The Cooperative Vulnerability](/inheritance/cooperating-under-bad-faith/cooperative-vulnerability) are also upstream inputs in the cases where the pattern that surfaces is one of those mechanisms specifically.

**Across the Inheritance.** Repairing After Rupture in the Bond is the practice the third condition refers to. The third condition's bar is high where repair is safe: it must then be attempted in full, not gestured at, not procedurally truncated. It never requires unsafe contact or a harmed person's participation. The Exclusion Problem cannot operate cleanly in a framework that has not developed Repairing After Rupture to operational depth. The two categories are sequentially linked: a framework strong at repair can rarely encounter the exclusion question; a framework weak at repair will encounter it routinely, often for the wrong reasons. Belonging Through Practice in the Bond develops the Compact's identity-and-belonging-through-practice architecture. The Exclusion Problem governs a different question: when continued participation in a particular cooperative setting has become incompatible with that setting's survival. The downstream boundary does not become the Compact's definition.

**Limitations.** Three worth naming. First, the tool cannot eliminate the false-positive risk. Even a four-condition audit can be wrong, and a framework that excludes inappropriately damages someone whose only fault was operating inside a framework that misread them. The tool's procedural intensity is the structural safeguard, not a guarantee. Second, the tool is itself vulnerable to weaponization by parties operating in bad faith — an asymmetric actor wielding the conditions audit against good-faith critics is the chronic risk the safeguards check is designed against, and no procedure makes the risk vanish. Third, exclusion does not solve the underlying problem of how to operate cooperatively under bad-faith pressure. It removes one participant from one cooperative setting. The same patterns that produced the case may reappear in that setting with a different participant, or in adjacent settings the excluded participant moves to. The tool addresses an acute case; the work of the rest of the category addresses the conditions that produce such cases.
